# Vigilant Crime — next long-form: British poverty (receipt format)

**Title (vidIQ 97):** Over 13 Million Britons Are in Poverty. Here's What a Week Looks Like
**Length:** about 4:00 at ElevenLabs pace (~620 words)
**Format:** one till receipt printing line by line; stat "stamps" land beside it; the final total is shown first.

## Voiceover (paste into ElevenLabs)

Minus twenty-two pounds.

That's not someone's bank balance. That's what's left at the end of a normal week, before they've paid a single penny of rent.

Over thirteen million people in the UK are living in poverty. So instead of telling you how bad it is, let's print it out.

Meet one person. Single. Over twenty-five. Out of work. On Universal Credit.

Line one. Money in.

The standard allowance this year is four hundred and twenty-four pounds ninety a month. Sounds like something you could live on, until you split it into weeks.

Ninety-eight pounds and five pence. That's the week. That's it.

Line two. Money out.

Two of the biggest anti-poverty organisations in the country, the Joseph Rowntree Foundation and the Trussell Trust, worked out what a single adult needs, at the very least, for food, household bills, clothes and the basics.

Not takeaways. Not subscriptions. The basics.

One hundred and twenty pounds a week.

So let's print the total.

Ninety-eight in. A hundred and twenty out.

Minus twenty-two pounds. Every single week. That's more than eleven hundred pounds a year that simply isn't there.

And rent isn't even on this receipt. That's a separate bill. The average private rent in the UK is now one thousand four hundred and six pounds a month.

Now multiply that receipt.

The Joseph Rowntree Foundation counted fourteen point two million people in poverty in 2023 to 24. More than one in five.

Six point eight million of them were in what it calls very deep poverty. The highest level in more than thirty years.

Three and a half million people in poverty can't afford enough food.

And here's the line that breaks the stereotype. Two thirds of working-age adults in poverty, five point four million people, live in a household where someone has a job.

Poverty in Britain isn't just people out of work. It's people at work.

Then there are the children.

Four and a half million in 2023 to 24. The newest government figures, using a new method, put it at around four million. That's more than one child in four.

Last year, food banks in the Trussell network handed out over two point six million emergency food parcels. More than nine hundred thousand of them were for children. That's the fourth year in a row above nine hundred thousand.

So is anything actually changing?

One line did change this year. In April, the two-child limit on benefits was scrapped. The Joseph Rowntree Foundation projects around four hundred thousand fewer children in poverty because of it.

That's real. But in October the energy price cap went up again. A typical home is now looking at around one thousand seven hundred and twenty-three pounds a year.

Here's my read. And it is just my read.

We talk about poverty like it's a number on the news. Fourteen million. Four million. It washes straight over you.

But it isn't a number. It's a receipt. It prints every week, and the total never changes.

Minus twenty-two. Before rent. Before the boiler breaks. Before Christmas.

You can argue about the fix. Benefits, wages, rent, all of it. But you can't argue with the maths.

So here's my question. If you had ninety-eight pounds for the week, what would you cut first?

Tell me in the comments. Every source is in the description.

- Standard allowance: most sources give £424.90/month (single, 25+, 2026/27); MoneySavingExpert shows £424.95. Confirm on gov.uk; £424.90 × 12 ÷ 52 = £98.05. If it's £424.95, the week is £98.07 and the voiceover line changes.
- Children: JRF's 4.5M (2023/24) and the ~4M / 27% for 2024/25 (CPAG reading of DWP data) use different methods. The script says so; keep it that way.
- The 400,000 fewer children is a JRF projection, not a measured result.
- The £120/week essentials figure excludes rent; the script says so.
- Energy £1,723 is Ofgem's typical dual-fuel Direct Debit household, 1 Oct to 31 Dec 2026.

## Part 2 voiceover (paste into ElevenLabs)

```
Part two.

Receipt number two belongs to a single parent.

Of every group the Joseph Rowntree Foundation tracks, working-age lone parents have the highest poverty rate. Thirty-nine percent. Almost four in ten.

It isn't hard to see why. One income. Childcare. School shoes. And every line on the receipt still has to be paid in full.

Receipt number three. Someone who's disabled.

Twenty-eight percent of disabled people are in poverty. That's about eight points higher than people who aren't disabled. And that gap doesn't close. It's there year after year.

And that's before the extra costs a lot of disabled people face just to get through the week.

Receipt number four. A pensioner.

Overall, about one pensioner in six is in poverty. But if you're a pensioner living alone, it's one in five.

This is the person deciding, every single evening, whether the heating goes on.

Now look at the address on these receipts. Because where you live changes everything.

The government's own local figures, after housing costs, show the share of children under sixteen growing up in poverty ranges from eight percent in East Dunbartonshire, in Scotland, to fifty percent in Tower Hamlets, in London.

Half. One child in every two.

London as a whole has the highest child poverty rate in England. Thirty-eight percent. The capital of one of the richest countries on earth, and it's the worst place in England to be a poor child.

And that's because of the line we still haven't printed. Housing.

At the end of March this year, one hundred and thirty-five thousand, five hundred and eighty households in England were living in temporary accommodation.

That can mean a hostel. A B and B. One room for a whole family. Somewhere that isn't home.

And one hundred and seventy-seven thousand, five hundred and thirty children were living there with them. The highest number on record.

That's enough children to fill Wembley Stadium. Very nearly twice.

Then there's the bill that doesn't show up until it's too late.

Energy debt.

Across Great Britain, people now owe their energy suppliers around five billion pounds. That's the figure from Ofgem and the government.

More than two million households are in debt or arrears on their energy.

And the industry's own trade body has warned it could pass seven billion by the end of this year.

Every one of those pounds started as a week where the receipt didn't add up, and the gap went on a bill instead.

And then there's the very bottom of the receipt. The part with nothing left on it.

The Joseph Rowntree Foundation has a word for it. Destitution. It means you can't afford the most basic things you need to stay warm, dry, clean and fed.

In its latest count, for 2022, three point eight million people in the UK experienced destitution. Around a million of them were children.

That's up sixty-one percent in three years.

And here's the line I can't get past. Almost three quarters of the people who were destitute were already getting benefits.

The safety net was there. They fell through it anyway.

So is any of this getting better?

Some of it. The two-child limit is gone. In September a new duty to support children in temporary accommodation came in. And a scheme to write off some older energy debt is due next year.

But here's the forecast. The Joseph Rowntree Foundation looked at the official projections and expects the overall poverty rate to stay basically flat. Twenty-one point three percent now. Twenty-one point one by 2029.

Flat. Not falling. Flat.

Their answer, together with the Trussell Trust, is something called an Essentials Guarantee. One simple rule: the basic rate of Universal Credit should never be lower than what the essentials actually cost. Food. Bills. The basics.

Right now, for a single adult, the gap between the two is the twenty-two pounds we started with.

Here's my read. And it is just my read.

We talk about poverty like it's a number on the news. Thirteen million. Four million. Five billion. It washes straight over you.

But it isn't a number. It's a stack of receipts. A single parent's. A pensioner's. A kid in one room in a B and B.

They print every week. And the totals barely move.

Minus twenty-two. Before rent. Before the boiler breaks. Before Christmas.

You can argue about the fix. Benefits, wages, rent, energy, all of it. But you can't argue with the maths. And nobody can say they didn't know.

So here's my question. If you had ninety-eight pounds for the week, what would you cut first?

Tell me in the comments. Every source is in the description.

This is Vigilant Crime. Subscribe for the next file.
```
