# Vigilant Crime — British poverty (receipt format), one continuous script

**Title (vidIQ 97):** Over 13 Million Britons Are in Poverty. Here's What a Week Looks Like
**Length:** 1246 words of voiceover, about 8.3 minutes at 150 words a minute (nearer 10 with music beats and pauses)
**Format:** one till receipt printing line by line, new receipts for each group, stat stamps beside them; the final total is shown first.

## Voiceover (paste into ElevenLabs)

```
Minus twenty-two pounds.

That's not a bank balance. It's what's left at the end of a normal week in Britain, before a single penny of rent.

Over thirteen million people in the UK are living in poverty. That number gets read out on the news, and nobody blinks.

So I'm not going to read it out. I'm going to print it.

And by the end, I'll show you the one line on this receipt that just got better, and why the total still won't move.

Meet one person. Single. Over twenty-five. Out of work. On Universal Credit.

Line one. Money in.

The standard allowance this year is four hundred and twenty-four pounds ninety a month. That sounds like something you could live on. Until you split it into weeks.

Ninety-eight pounds and five pence. That's the whole week.

Line two. Money out.

The Joseph Rowntree Foundation and the Trussell Trust, two of the biggest anti-poverty charities in the country, worked out the bare minimum a single adult needs for food, household bills, clothes and basics.

Not takeaways. Not subscriptions. Not a night out.

One hundred and twenty pounds a week.

Ninety-eight in. A hundred and twenty out.

Minus twenty-two pounds. Every single week. Over a year, that's more than eleven hundred pounds that simply isn't there.

And rent isn't even on this receipt. That's a separate bill. The average private rent in the UK is now one thousand four hundred and six pounds a month.

So how many receipts look like this?

The government's latest count is thirteen point four million people in relative poverty, after housing costs. One person in five.

The Joseph Rowntree Foundation's count for the year before was even higher. Fourteen point two million.

And almost half of them, six point eight million people, were in what it calls very deep poverty. The highest level in more than thirty years.

Three and a half million people in poverty can't afford enough food.

Now here's the part most people get wrong.

You probably pictured the person on this receipt as someone who doesn't work.

But two thirds of working-age adults in poverty, five point four million people, live in a household where someone has a job.

Poverty in Britain isn't just people out of work. It's people at work.

And the person on our receipt isn't even the one struggling most.

Print the next one. A single parent.

Of every group the Joseph Rowntree Foundation tracks, working-age lone parents have the highest poverty rate. Thirty-nine percent. Almost four in ten.

One income. Childcare. School shoes. And every line still has to be paid in full.

Next receipt. Someone who's disabled.

Twenty-eight percent of disabled people are in poverty. About eight points higher than people who aren't. And that gap has been there year after year.

That's before the extra costs a lot of disabled people face just to get through the week.

Next. A pensioner.

About one pensioner in six is in poverty. If they live alone, it's one in five.

This is the person deciding, every evening, whether the heating goes on.

Then there are the children.

Four and a half million in 2023 to 24. The newest government figures, using a new method, put it at around four million. Either way, that's more than one child in four.

Last year, food banks in the Trussell network handed out more than two point six million emergency food parcels.

Over nine hundred thousand of them were for children.

The fourth year in a row above nine hundred thousand.

And where those children live changes everything.

The government's own local figures, after housing costs, show the share of children under sixteen in poverty ranges from eight percent in East Dunbartonshire, in Scotland, to fifty percent in Tower Hamlets, in London.

Half. One child in every two.

London as a whole has the highest child poverty rate in England. Thirty-eight percent.

The capital of one of the richest countries on earth.

And the reason is the line we still haven't printed. Housing.

At the end of March, one hundred and thirty-five thousand, five hundred and eighty households in England were living in temporary accommodation.

A hostel. A B and B. One room for a whole family. Somewhere that isn't home.

Living there with them, one hundred and seventy-seven thousand, five hundred and thirty children.

The highest number on record.

That's enough children to fill Wembley Stadium. Very nearly twice.

Then there's the bill that arrives after the damage is done.

In October, the energy price cap went up again. A typical home is now looking at around one thousand seven hundred and twenty-three pounds a year.

And across Great Britain, people already owe their energy suppliers around five billion pounds. That's the figure from Ofgem and the government.

More than two million households are in energy debt or arrears.

The industry's own trade body has warned it could pass seven billion by the end of this year.

Every one of those pounds started as a week where the receipt didn't add up, and the gap went on a bill instead.

Which brings us to the very bottom of the receipt. The part with nothing left on it.

The Joseph Rowntree Foundation has a word for it. Destitution. It means you can't afford the basics to stay warm, dry, clean and fed.

In its latest count, for 2022, three point eight million people in the UK experienced destitution. Around a million of them were children.

That was up sixty-one percent in three years.

And here's the line I can't get past.

Almost three quarters of the people who were destitute were already getting benefits.

The safety net was there. They fell through it anyway.

So, I promised you the line that just got better.

In April, the two-child limit on benefits was scrapped. The Joseph Rowntree Foundation projects around four hundred thousand fewer children in poverty because of it.

In September, a new duty to support children in temporary accommodation came in. And a scheme to write off some older energy debt is due next year.

That's real progress.

But now look at the forecast.

The Joseph Rowntree Foundation looked at the official projections, and expects the overall poverty rate to stay basically flat. Twenty-one point three percent now. Twenty-one point one by 2029.

Flat. Not falling. Flat.

Their fix, with the Trussell Trust, is called an Essentials Guarantee. One rule: the basic rate of Universal Credit should never be lower than what the essentials actually cost.

And for a single adult right now, the gap between those two numbers is exactly where we started.

Twenty-two pounds.

Here's my read. And it is just my read.

We talk about poverty like it's a number on the news. Thirteen million. Four million. Five billion. It washes straight over you.

But it isn't a number. It's a stack of receipts. A single parent's. A pensioner's. A kid in one room in a B and B.

They print every week. And the totals barely move.

Minus twenty-two. Before rent. Before the boiler breaks. Before Christmas.

You can argue about the fix. Benefits, wages, rent, energy, all of it.

But you can't argue with the maths. And nobody can say they didn't know.

So here's my question. If you had ninety-eight pounds for the week, what would you cut first?

Tell me in the comments. Every source is in the description.

This is Vigilant Crime. Subscribe for the next file.
```

## Check before recording

- Standard allowance: most sources give £424.90/month (single, 25+, 2026/27); MoneySavingExpert shows £424.95. Confirm on gov.uk. At £424.95 the week is £98.07.
- Children: 4.5M (2023/24) and ~4M (2024/25) use different methods; the script says so.
- The 400,000 fewer children is a JRF projection, not a measured result.
- Lone parents 39%, disabled 28% (2023/24), pensioners 16% overall and 21% living alone: JRF figures; label them "latest figures" where the year isn't stated.
- Tower Hamlets: DWP table 50%, Save the Children 50.3%; the script says "half".
- Destitution is 2022 data from JRF's 2023 report, the latest found; update it if a newer edition is out.
- Energy debt: Ofgem and the government give about £5bn (Q2 2026); Energy UK's higher £6bn uses a different measure, and the £7bn is a warning.
- Wembley: 177,530 ÷ 90,000 capacity = 1.97, hence "very nearly twice".
